“This is how journalism has always been done”: White neutrality, newsroom norms, and an AI intervention in source diversification

Gavin Adamson https://orcid.org/0000-0002-8846-3047 [email protected], Asmaa Malik https://orcid.org/0000-0003-0570-3963, […], Shari Okeke, and Jisele Bayley-Hay+1-1View all authors and affiliations

Abstract

This study draws on interviews with 12 journalists in a Canadian newsroom to examine how they understand source diversification, how they perceive AI-based source-tracking technology, and how their reflections illuminate discourse on race and accountability in journalism. Based on semi-structured interviews conducted after a demonstration of the technology, the analysis identifies the motivations and barriers shaping source diversification, including professional norms, representational goals, time constraints, and institutional culture. Reporters express commitments to inclusive sourcing, but remain constrained by routines that privilege official voices, anticipated by research analysis like framing and indexing theories. Participants describe the AI tool, which quantitatively scores sources according to their relationship to societal power structures, as a potential catalyst for reflective practice and accountability. They also suggest the technology may sit uneasily within professional norms. The study is contextualized in research about role performance and race in newsrooms. Finally, this AI-based source-tracking technology resituates journalism within a Bourdieusian field, pulled toward social and cultural capital in relation to structural racism.

Introduction

Source diversification – counting and categorizing sources, then intervening to amplify historically marginalized voices – has been promoted as a way to strengthen transparency and rebuild public trust. It is also a practice–research gap in journalism despite long-standing concerns about whose voices shape news coverage (Wilner et al., 2025). This study examines newsroom motivations and barriers to tracking source diversity using an Artificial Intelligence-based technology designed to categorize sources by power hierarchies. The development of the tool was inspired by sociological research about sourcing, related to the role sources play in activating frames, and by indexing theory, which suggests news narratives are heavily led by sources that reflect political status quo. The goal was to produce a quantitative-based technology that may inspire newsroom reflection and provide transparency in journalism production – a social science heuristic for newsrooms.
The context includes attention to equity and representation following George Floyd’s murder, a “pivot point” confronting journalistic authority (Schmidt, 2023: p 552). His murder at the hands of a white police officer in Minnesota was documented on video by 17-year-old Darnella Frazier, earning her a special Pulitzer citation for capturing the violent act that “spurred protests against police brutality around the world” (The Pulitzer Prizes, 2021).
In the months that followed, the participating newsroom in this study publicly acknowledged shortcomings in its coverage of Black, racialized, and Indigenous communities, noting that greater diversity could improve reporting. In 2020, the newsroom partnered with two co-authors of this study to apply for funding supporting the development of a source-tracking tool. The technology uses natural language processing (NLP) to categorize sources into seven groups: politicians, experts, authorities, organizations, celebrities, media and unaffiliated (Adamson et al., 2025). It was demonstrated to staff and considered by them for potential use in the newsroom ahead of their interviews.
Twelve journalists and editors participated in individual, semi-structured interviews exploring their sourcing practices, reporting experiences, and views on integrating this AI into their newsroom. (We have obscured the funding source and the news organization to anonymize reporters as per our research protocol.) Participants described motivations ranging from responding to Canada’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s Calls to Action, (Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Canada, 2015) to improving sourcing. They also identified barriers rooted in norms, time pressure, and limited editorial discussion about “diversity.” The results are timely. Budarick argues that recent appeals to impartiality as the solution for journalism, post-Floyd, are entwined with racism structurally (2023).
This research pulls together framing and indexing theory into consideration with scholarship on race in journalism, objectivity, and role performance. It suggests sourcing is partly a racialized practice through which news organizations reproduce judgments about whose knowledge counts as journalistically legitimate. The AI-based source-tracking tool can quantify hierarchies quickly and make them visible. In the context of the research, it also underlines institutionalized hierarchies that underwrite professional norms of objectivity embedded partly in the historical whiteness of newsrooms.
Through field theory, which researchers have used several times to interrogate race and representation, we conceptualize the AI tool as a potential intervention in the journalistic field, previously pulled toward forms of Bourdieuian economic capital through the rise of another technology, web analytics (Tandoc, 2014). Participant interviews suggest the automated source-diversity metric examined here potentially draws the field in the direction of social, cultural, and symbolic capital. Their responses indicate tensions, including what kinds of cultural knowledge and accountability practices can be recognized as valuable journalism, autonomously identified and valued within the profession.

Literature review

Artificial Intelligence, technology and journalism

Artificial intelligence has been used in journalism long before ChatGPT intensified attention to its capacity to transform newsrooms. Earlier computational and automated journalism tools supported editorial decision-making, production, distribution, fact-checking, audience personalization, and discoverability (Noain-Sánchez, 2022). Researchers have examined these systems in relation to journalistic roles, organizational structures, content production, and audience relationships (Löffelholz and Sarisakaloglu, 2025; Pavlik, 2025). Economically, automation has often been framed as reducing routine work and freeing journalists for higher-value reporting. Professionally, however, AI also affects journalistic values and norms as use cases expand (Löffelholz and Sarisakaloglu, 2025). Newsrooms adopt these systems not only for efficiency, but also because AI experimentation signals innovation, attracts institutional attention, and offers a way to renegotiate journalism’s authority amid economic and technological uncertainty. In moments of technological “hype,” news organizations renegotiate professional autonomy and retest assumptions about journalistic role performance (Lewis et al., 2025).
NLP has been an important strand of AI use in journalism, especially for text classification, extraction, summarization, pattern recognition, and automated writing. Some earlier forms of automated journalism used structured data and natural language generation to produce routine texts, such as sports recaps and financial summaries (Noain-Sánchez, 2022; Pavlik, 2025). The AI-based sourcing technology examined here belongs to this broader NLP tradition, but it was trained for a limited task using a pre-GPT large language model (LLM): identifying, classifying, and counting journalism sources in text in ways recognizable to researchers and news consumers. Its purpose is to make disproportionate sourcing patterns visible. The tool classifies sources by public role rather than by race, gender, or other demographic characteristics, and it does not require storing race or gender data. To our knowledge, no other study has placed this kind of AI-based source-tracking technology into a newsroom context to examine how journalists and editors understand its implications for practice.
Since ChatGPT’s release, journalism and journalism education have focused intensely on generative AI and LLMs; NLP refers to computational techniques for processing language; LLMs are one class of language model; and generative AI describes systems, often built on LLMs, that produce new text, images, audio, or other outputs. These systems can generate, summarize, classify, translate, and transform text across contexts, and they can be prompted to identify bias or misinformation (Löffelholz and Sarisakaloglu, 2025). At the same time, they raise legal, ethical, and professional concerns. Scholars and practitioners have called for transparent and accountable AI use in journalism (Diakopoulos, 2015; Shin, 2022). Wiik (2025), drawing on the Partnership on AI in Europe, describes newsroom goals such as standards, explainability, accountability, staff supervision, and the integration of journalistic values into platform systems. Yet these goals are difficult to realize when newsrooms rely on third-party systems whose training data, infrastructure, and decision-making processes they do not control (Wiik, 2025). LLMs are trained on vast datasets that may include copyrighted journalism and sensitive personal information, raising concerns about source protection, privacy, and data breaches. Hallucination, classification error, and embedded bias further complicate journalistic reliance on these tools (Diakopoulos, 2015; Löffelholz and Sarisakaloglu, 2025). Generative AI introduces these technological challenges, but also an epistemic opportunity for journalism to reconsider its authority and knowledge practices (Perreault et al., 2025). This project examines one specific site of that reconsideration: the use of AI not to produce journalism, but to expose how newsroom routines make some voices legible and legitimate while leaving others harder to recognize.

Theorizing AI-based source technology in the journalism field

Sourcing is foundational to journalism. Researchers have examined who is excluded and how omissions shape public knowledge. Reporters rely on familiarity, legitimacy, and professional norms that privilege certain voices. Tuchman’s (1978) concept of objectivity explains why official perspectives dominate, reinforced by routines (Franklin and Carlson, 2010; Zelizer et al., 2022). “Information subsidies,” a term coined by Gandy (1982), described how political and corporate communications professionals package information for journalists to raise their agendas. It is effective, especially when timelines for producing content are tight and newsroom resources are scant (Wheatley, 2020). Community standards also shape which voices gain authority (Berkowitz and TerKeurst, 1999). Two conceptual frameworks inform this study because they each place the counting and classification of sources at the centre of their methodology. Framing analysis often suggests how journalists emphasize particular aspects of reality through the selection of sources whose perspectives anchor a narrative (Brüggemann, 2018; Entman, 1993; Matthes and Strömbäck, 2018). Source categories often align with particular frames (Mercado Sáez, 2022), and actors may sponsor frames that guide coverage (Wichgers et al., 2021). Indexing theory similarly suggests that news tends to mirror the perspectives of political elites and authorities (Bennett, 1990, 2019; Livingston and Bennett, 2003). Together, framing and indexing show how institutional sources enjoy structural advantages that shape coverage of all kinds (Berkowitz, 2019).
Bourdieu’s field theory has been deployed in several studies about marginalized sources in journalism. These studies provide insight into why and how official and institutional sources dominate the news, while others experience more friction. To review, in this theory, journalists’ disposition toward sources rests in a web of several forms of capital – economic capital (the need to be profitable), cultural capital (showing expertise and competence), social capital (influence with networks), and symbolic capital (reputation, and legitimacy in the eyes of its assumed audiences) (Tandoc, 2014). Broussard’s field-theoretical analysis of social-political stories in the sports beat suggests such stories are pursued sometimes because of mutually shared cultural capital between reporters and athletes – in particular when each understands race and marginalization (Broussard, 2020). In an illustrative moment, the research points to the 2016 NFL player protests about police brutality. “Colin Kaepernick, when he gives his first big sit-down interview, it’s not going to be to Scott Pelley of 60 Minutes,” said one participant (p.1638). If social-political concerns are not shared between source and reporter, they are seen as outside pressure rather than part of the profession’s habitus. These notions are foundational to discussions about source diversity and its acceptance in newsrooms. Field theory also helps explain the impact of technology.
For more than a decade, web analytics, the real-time tracking of audience interest in online content, has been studied thoroughly in the context of field theory, a useful lens for understanding how an AI-based source-tracking could land in a newsroom. Technology is never neutral; interpreted through existing professional norms, it can reconfigure journalism’s field (Tandoc, 2014). The changes in tensions between the forms of capital described above can be disruptive and meaningful. Tandoc argues the shift from print to online advertising increased the influence of economic capital, with some newsrooms pursuing clickable stories to drive revenue. Editors may defend news sense and public interest despite performance metrics, but analytics affect story selection and “de-selection,” the removal of stories likely to underperform (Duffy et al., 2018). Source-tracking is a newer consideration, but field theory suggests that relationships with more diverse source networks could animate new forms of social and cultural capital that become part of the profession’s autonomy.
The technology described in this research was developed over several years, as parallel action around source diversity has gathered. Many news organizations now track pronouns, geography, gender, and race (Grannan et al., 2024; Campion-Smith, 2022; Lau, 2021; Nature, 2024). Tools such as API’s Source Matters and the DIANES toolkit offer automated categorization of sources. Source Matters automatically identifies and extracts the names of individuals quoted in published news stories, after which reporters log each source’s demographic information (race, gender, age, etc.). DIANES (Diversity Auditor for News Sources) is a real-time DEI audit toolkit developed by researchers that uses an NLP pipeline to extract quotes, speakers, titles, and organizations from news articles and then predicts each speaker’s gender and race/ethnicity (Shang et al., 2022). A tool built by Chalkbeat and the Reynolds Journalism Institute asks journalists to enter a source’s name and email, after which the tool sends a demographic survey to the source. Individual journalists can access their own data; newsroom leaders can view aggregate numbers and diversity breakdowns (Bauman, 2021). Resolve Philly’s initiative, Modifier, focuses on “equitable news frames” and a source-tracking protocol that emphasizes reporter-led data collection during reporting (Modifier, 2023).
Yet collecting demographic data raises concerns about privacy, consent, and comfort, particularly among marginalized groups (Lau, 2021). These are compounded by the history of demographic data being weaponized against marginalized communities, giving people well-founded reasons for caution when asked to disclose identity-related information to news organizations (Partnership on AI, 2024). Racialized sources often appear primarily in coverage explicitly about race (Wenzel, 2023), and focus groups across four countries found participants wary of tokenism and performative diversity efforts (Toff et al., 2024).
Nevertheless, resistance to adopting some source tracking efforts may reflect general findings on journalistic role performance. Reporters testing Source Matters, for example, struggled with the extra time required for recording sources (Vu, 2023). Journalism’s closed professional culture can resist external scrutiny (Waisbord, 2013, 2017), and transparency tools may conflict with professional ideals (Mellado, 2015; Mellado et al., 2020). The AI-based source-tracking technology at the centre of this study addresses some challenges. It eliminates the reporters’ time required to record sources and the privacy concerns that come with them, especially for marginalized sources. Research using the methodology used in this study suggests entrenched imbalances, such as the dominance of police and legal sources in mental illness coverage (Adamson and Donaldson, 2025) and the political elites in coverage related to Indigenous communities (Adamson et al., 2025). Each of the studies suggested statistical over-representation of certain kinds of sources in these news subjects compared to a benchmark.

Race, power, and newsroom decision-making

While “diversity” is a stated goal for many North American newsrooms, research shows a chasm between inclusive rhetoric and editorial norms, not the least because the vagueness of the stand-alone term itself leads to numerous mutable definitions. Decision-making remains concentrated in white leadership (Nishikawa et al., 2009). Racialized journalists describe acting as informal “sensitivity readers” but encounter resistance when their perspectives conflict with dominant white norms (Clark, 2022 p. 90). Wenzel (2021) found that all story-shaping editors at the public radio station that was the subject of her study were white, limiting the influence of journalists of colour. Newsrooms often revert to safe interpretations of objectivity when dealing with stories about race, prioritizing neutrality over meaningful engagement (Schmidt, 2023). Canadian newsrooms remain overwhelmingly white, with more than 83.7% of supervisors identifying as white and more than 69.1% reporting no visible minorities at all in leadership (Canadian Association of Journalists, 2025). In Canada, the proportion of white columnists increased over the past two decades, reversing the national demographic trend (Fatah et al., 2026). These imbalances may restrict news decisions because white reporters, when covering race, often “fall back on traditional, passive practices of objectivity such as deferring to official sources and remaining separate from communities” (Robinson and Culver, 2019: p. 375). They argue that norms such as objectivity keep power systems in place because the concept is rooted in white-dominated institutions. Journalists who are Black, Indigenous, and people of colour have noted the invisible labour involved in including more sources of colour and discussing sources with editors – efforts they initiate “pretty much 100% of the time” (Wenzel, 2023: p. 65). Budarick argues that renewed appeals to impartiality as journalism’s solution post-Floyd risks returning to an idealized, white liberal way of knowing that is intertwined with racism rather than external to it (2023). He suggests that this status quo approach locates whiteness as neutral. It places racism in the hands of extremists rather than in structures. It situates decolonial or anti-oppressive stances as advocacy.
Unsurprisingly, audits show that sourcing often skews white (Wenzel, 2021). Racialized journalists attempt to diversify sourcing but face gatekeeping and inconsistent institutional support (Bramlett-Solomon et al., 2025; Mattar, 2020). Efforts to diversify expert lists or outreach efforts remain symbolic unless integrated into workflows, which prioritize sources who return journalists’ calls quickly. Norms of neutrality privilege institutional perspectives and position community knowledge as biased (Alamo-Pastrana and Hoynes, 2018; Tuchman, 1972). This stance treats belief in structural racism as external to “good journalism.” Reliance on official sources appears neutral even when it returns to hierarchies (Budarick, 2023). The challenges above involve representation and newsroom demographics, but also concerns about what is considered neutral, legitimate or contestable within journalism. Counting and categorizing sources alone cannot solve but ignoring them has deeper consequences. Coverage about Indigenous and First Nations communities in Canada is a case in point.

Indigenous representation in Canadian media

The editor of the participating newsroom in this study publicly committed to better coverage of the city’s Indigenous population, so it is important to review research on the marginalization and stereotyping of Indigenous peoples in Canadian journalism (King and Odartey-Wellington, 2022). Anderson and Robertson’s Seeing Red (2011) traces patterns to colonial portrayals. News coverage tends to emphasize poverty, violence, and dysfunction, reinforcing stereotypes rather than presenting balanced narratives (Callison and Young, 2020). Portrayals cast Indigenous groups as threats and Indigenous-led activism as disruptive (Cote, 2021; Wilkes, 2011). Indigenous issues have historically been covered by non-Indigenous reporters, though individual Indigenous journalists have reframed narratives through a variety of journalistic platforms (Callison and Young, 2020). Indigenous-led outlets such as Aboriginal People’s Television Network provide counter-narratives and context related to Indigenous self-government, land rights and protection, while outlets such as the CBC and CTV frame Indigenous action in relation to conflict, traffic disruption and police intervention (Mongibello, 2019). As mentioned the source-tracking technology used in this study suggests that political voices are statistically overrepresented in news about Indigenous communities.
Considering the complex mix of technological and sociological concerns, we sought to answer three questions: First, how do journalists and editors in a mid-sized Canadian newsroom understand the value and challenges of diversifying sources? Second, how do they perceive the role of an AI-based source tracking technology? Third, in what ways do participants view the technology as supporting more reflexive thinking in the newsroom about race, representation, and power?

Methodology

This study was conducted by four researchers, three with more than 15 years of newsroom experience each. Two identify as Black, one brown and one white, and as cis, heterosexual, and settler. These positionalities shaped interpretations of sourcing practices. These positionalities shaped interpretations of sourcing practices. Goundar (2025) suggests researchers can see work through “insider-outsider” lenses with respect to their subjects. Although we are now outside the profession, we have insider experience as journalists and student journalists who understand its norms. Yet two of us have experience as racialized participants within predominantly white Canadian newsrooms (as described above), arguably as insiders also on the outside.
The participating newsroom, a mid-sized Canadian daily newsroom serving an urban market with sizable Indigenous communities, agreed to partner with the researchers through an external innovation funding program. The funding allowed the researchers to hire an external AI development team.
For privacy reasons described above, the platform was not designed to determine race, gender, or other demographics. Instead, it was trained to identify sources by categories that a variety of researcher paradigms suggest are over-represented in reporting. We developed an NLP pipeline to identify and categorize sources into seven categories: political, authority, expert, organization, unaffiliated, media, and celebrity. A similar source categorization approach was employed by Poindexter et al. (2003). It was modified, described and deployed in two research studies (Adamson and Donaldson, 2026; Adamson et al., 2025) that showed statistically valid results. Because this research involved the coverage of First Nations and Indigenous populations in the province (as per research protocol guidance), we sought consultation with these communities; two groups responded and consented to sharing their recommendations on the broader project. Following ethics approval, all editorial staff were invited to an anonymous, 45 to 60-min individual interview. Twelve journalists and editors participated and received a gift card honorarium. Via video interface, participants watched the platform ingest article text, identify sources, and place them into categories within seconds. They asked questions and spent time understanding the interface and its purpose. Interviewers’ questions asked participants to describe how they select sources, or, for editors, how they assess and advise on source selection. Questions also addressed sourcing across communities different from the journalist’s own, the considerations that shape decisions about whom to contact, and how participants understood the professional obligations involved in source choice. After viewing the platform demonstration, participants were asked where in the reporting and publishing process the tool might be most useful, how they might use it in reporting or editing, what effects it could have on the newsroom, and what concerns they had about newsroom use, public availability, or the possibility that source-tracking could operate like other newsroom metrics technologies, including web analytics.
Rather than relying only on a saturation or redundancy threshold, we considered the sample through the concept of information power (Malterud et al., 2016). Twelve interviews may be judged sufficient because the study’s aim was focused, the participant group was specific to one newsroom, the interviews were anchored in a shared demonstration of the source-tracking technology, and the analysis was guided by established theory about sourcing, field theory, role performance, and race in newsrooms. This rationale is consistent with Ahmed’s (2025) discussion of sample adequacy in relatively homogeneous qualitative samples. Interviews were conducted primarily via video call across November and December 2023. One interview was completed by telephone with a participant who had previously seen the technology in action. Because this study examines a small professional community, our approach to confidentiality emphasizes risk reduction rather than a guarantee of anonymity. We have not named the newsroom, city, province, funding program, participating Indigenous communities, or technology vendors. We also avoid identifying participants by beat, job title, demographic profile, or seniority unless directly relevant to the analysis. Some contextual details have been generalized, and some quotations have been lightly edited or paraphrased to preserve analytic meaning while reducing the possibility of deductive disclosure. Participants also reviewed and approved their transcripts.
Anonymized transcripts were coded collaboratively by members of the research team in a spreadsheet. This platform allowed visual mapping of codes and themes and iterative refinement of the thematic structure. All textual data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s approach (2019), a six-step process: (1) familiarization with the data through close reading of all transcripts; (2) generating initial codes and identifying features relevant to the research questions; (3) collating codes into potential themes and grouping related codes to capture broader patterns across participants; (4) reviewing themes to ensure that each theme reflects the data and is distinct from others; (5) defining and naming themes and refining the scope and focus of each; and (6) reporting findings and presenting the themes with supporting examples from the data. After the researchers met with the newsroom leader to describe some of their results, the newsroom decided it would not participate in the development of the platform.

Findings and discussion

Participants, each of whom is quoted below in as few as one but as many as four different places, identified four recurring barriers to source diversification: time pressure, routine reliance on official sources, challenges of access and representation, and weak institutional emphasis on diverse sourcing. Reflecting field theory, this suggests that source concentration is reproduced through newsroom habitus and through professional role expectations about what counts as credible journalism. One participant described searching past coverage for “people who have already spoken with the media” because “it is just a lot faster,” a way of “managing my time.” Some sources remain harder to recognize, reach, and justify within normal newswork. The source-tracking tool was not imagined as a solution to this condition. More often, participants imagined it as a technology that could make routines visible.
Time pressure was the least surprising barrier. Almost all participants described the demand to “churn things out quickly” narrowed the window to reach new voices. Participants did not say the AI technology would alter that condition during production. Instead, they imagined using the tool retroactively, reviewing content by article or by corpus. “Having that time to analyze it and say, what can we do better next time?” would be valuable, said one reporter. Journalistic role performance research suggests interventions are consequential if they are folded into routines of evaluation and accountability.
The more theoretically important finding relates to the dominance of official and institutional sources. Participants repeatedly described ministries, police, academic experts, and other recognized authorities as the default sources. One reporter said that lighter stories allow more casual interviews, but “serious issues” tend to default to experts, while another summarized the logic: contacting a ministry or government spokesperson is often “quicker than going out to the community.” Another participant explained official-source dominance as a structural problem of shrinking newsrooms and information access: police and government sources are “gatekeeping this information,” leaving reporters “increasingly relying on these official sources.” In this context, a review of 1 month’s coverage about some communities could “show me my patterns,” suggested one reporter. Another said such analysis could, “facilitate more conversation,” asking whether journalists who spend all day speaking with sources are “really talking to each other as much as we probably should be.” Framing and indexing theory were plain in the interview transcripts. One participant said police reports are literally the beginning of news stories and in later versions police anchor the terms through which a story is understood.
The literature about race and newsrooms suggests these norms are also shaped within white-dominant leadership structures that continue to define whose knowledge is trusted and what’s routine. As a motivation to use AI-source tracking, participants often described its value as diagnostic of those routines. One participant imagined it as a reminder to “call the people actually affected, not just the people in power,” while another reporter said it would drive calls to sources, “beyond the professor or minister’s spokesperson.” Several participants described unaffiliated or lived-experience sources as central to good reporting. One reporter called such sources “the gold standard,” preferring “someone with lived experience” and “no strings attached.” The tool could not undo indexing, but it could make it visible inside newsroom workflows and the extent to which official voices frame a great majority of stories. Findings on access to sources, through newsroom representation, set the limits of how much the source-tracking technology could intervene. Reporters described source diversification from unfamiliar communities as slow, relational work that depends on trust, repeated contact, and cultural competence. One reporter said such efforts depend on “communities being willing to talk,” while another described sourcing in unfamiliar communities as a “two-stage process” of getting to know the source, then interviewing them. Embedded in these responses is that community knowledge is more easily marked as partial, risky, or advocacy, partly because of the time involved in gaining trust not-earned yet. One participant said they had learned that asking marginalized people to serve as sources can impose burdens on them. Journalists should not rely on “minorities and people outside of your own community to be educators,” they said, adding that careful context-building is “a luxury of time” newsrooms often lack. They also clarify why source access is structured before quotation: not only by deadlines, but by whether journalists have the background knowledge, trust, and network ties needed to recognize a source as credible and be recognized by the source in return as capable of handling the issue properly. One reporter described his own positionality as white and older. He said the Indigenous community did not tend to trust him. Broussard’s work in the sports beat suggested how shared social understanding between source and reporter can deliver news differently.
Related to this, some reporters’ comments reveal the limits of diversity as a representational routine. One described seeking out “Indigenous community people,” and sources from “other walks of life” at a Santa Claus parade, while an editor said that, in a routine restaurant story, asking for a restaurant “owned by a person of colour,” might not “seem relevant.” The same editor added that the newsroom “wouldn’t necessarily even know” whether a restaurant was owned by that person. These comments point to the same professional tension: race is sometimes understood as a visible attribute to be added to a story, and at other times as irrelevant or unknowable unless explicitly named by the assignment. The editor’s admission that the newsroom “wouldn’t necessarily even know” names an institutional gap. The issue can sometimes be that journalists do not add racialized sources to otherwise complete stories, but also that predominantly white newsrooms may lack the relationships and background knowledge needed to recognize when race is relevant, where community authority resides, or what stories are being missed as described by Wenzel. The tool can make visible what kinds of sources appear in published stories. And it can only identify communities the newsroom does not know well enough to imagine as sources, institutions, or story subjects to the extent that politicians, authorities and experts may dominate some subjects structurally, as the two aforementioned case studies related to the tool suggest. As another reporter pointed out, it cannot capture “the calls you make that do not get returned.” The AI can identify who is missing from the finished story, but not the relational labour, failed outreach, or absence of trust that made them difficult to include.
Tension emerged from the interviews about institutional culture. Many participants said source diversity is not consistently embedded in newsroom expectations, and their commitment to it was down to individual choice and experience. One reporter said, “the newsroom doesn’t always push for it,” while a second said some colleagues may conclude, “it isn’t worth the extra effort, it is just not part of the culture.” Wenzel’s work on journalistic role performance theory suggests reporters hold certain values that are enacted unevenly within organizational limits. A reporter said the technology should do what editors already should: ask whose voice is missing, while another said it could “start conversations we never really have about sourcing.” Several participants described an ethical obligation to counter what one called the “incredibly white spaces” of Canadian newsrooms and to, “make better on our responsibilities as journalists to represent our entire community.” This reporter invoked the Truth and Reconciliation Commission’s 2015 report, which includes Calls to Action for the journalism industry for better coverage and participation by Indigenous people in the media industry. Another said reporters should, “highlight non-white voices because the newsroom is very white.” In that respect, reporters described how the tool could reshape role performance by making source diversity defensible as good editorial practice rather than as an add-on.
The literature above suggests that appeals to objectivity and impartiality functionally structurally to protect white ways of knowing, making efforts to treat structural racism as real appear ideological rather than journalistic. One reporter rejected the notion that the newsroom did not represent marginalized voices properly. Participants echoed resistance anticipated by the literature, too. One reporter suggested, “traditionalists in the newsroom would say, ‘we are always going to have more authorities, that is reflective of our coverage,’ and another one predicted some senior editors would dismiss the tool by insisting, “this is how journalism has always been done.” All participants saw the comparison of the source-tracking tool to web analytics: something reviewed after publication, discussed in meetings, and used to inform decisions rather than dictate reporting in real time. While analytics make audience attention visible as economic capital, source tracking may elevate a different form of value: professional competence in recognizing overreliance on official voices and narrow institutional framing. Participants said these logics could conflict. One reporter described the tool as “double-edged:” metrics become risky when they “direct what they’re actually reporting.” This tech could have the “same benefit, but also the same flaw” as web analytics. The technology, then, may not simply measure practice but intensify tensions among competing forms of capital in the journalistic field.
Nearly all participants supported public visibility of the technology and its results, but they expressed understandable ambivalence. The findings indicate that the tool’s value lies in making routine indexing toward official voices more visible; its limits are also clear when diversification depends on trust, newsroom demographics, and the accumulation of social and cultural capital. When participants suggested that the technology could stretch the profession in a way comparable to web analytics, they did not talk about audience and market response, but about making source diversity part of what counts as competent and defensible role performance.

Conclusion and limitations

This study examined how journalists and editors in a Canadian newsroom understand source diversification and how they perceive the role of an AI-based source-tracking tool in relation to professional norms, daily routines, and institutional culture. The findings show that participants generally valued more inclusive sourcing and saw it as important to accuracy, fairness, and public accountability. At the same time, they described persistent barriers including time pressure, reliance on official and institutional voices, uneven access to communities, and newsroom cultures in which diverse sourcing is not always consistently expected or supported. These findings suggest that sourcing patterns are shaped not simply by individual choice, but by entrenched professional routines and organizational assumptions about credibility, authority, and neutrality.
The study also found that participants did not view the AI-based source-tracking tool primarily as a real-time reporting aid. Rather, they understood it as a retrospective and reflective mechanism that could make sourcing routines visible, prompt editorial conversations, and support accountability around who is quoted. In this sense, the tool was seen less as a compliance device than as a potential catalyst for institutional reflexivity to “hold journalists to account” as one reporter put it. Returning to the comparison between AI-based source tracking and the earlier arrival of web analytics, framed through field theory, this kind of tool has the potential to stretch internal newsroom dynamics toward cultural and symbolic capital.
These contributions should be considered alongside several limitations. First, the study is based on a small sample of 12 participants in a single Canadian newsroom, which limits the generalizability of the findings. The sample provided sufficient information power for the study’s focused aim and bounded newsroom context, but that information power derives from specificity rather than breadth. Accordingly, the findings are best understood as analytically transferable to similar newsroom contexts, not generalizable across journalism as a whole. Second, the study relies on interview data gathered after a demonstration of the technology, so the findings reflect participants’ perceptions and anticipated uses of the tool rather than long-term observation of its adoption. Third, because the study did not collect participant demographic information in order to protect anonymity, it cannot assess how perspectives may have varied across racialized identities, roles, or levels of seniority. Finally, the tool itself categorizes sources by role but it cannot capture unsuccessful outreach, relationship-building labor, or the broader social conditions that shape who is available, trusted, and ultimately included.
Overall, the study shows that the challenges of source diversification are deeply cultural and institutional. AI-based source tracking may help newsrooms identify and reflect on imbalances in sourcing, but technology alone cannot overcome the structural routines, professional norms, and power relations that reproduce those imbalances. For source diversification to become a meaningful and sustained part of journalistic practice, it must be embedded in editorial expectations, supported by newsroom leadership, and understood as part of journalistic quality itself.
