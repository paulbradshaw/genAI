# PEER game: questions and answers

## Level 1: Know your PEER (1 pts each)

### 1.1 Spate of catalytic converter thefts hits market town (Crime, News)

- Source: Chief Inspector Nadia Rahman, local police
- Ask: "What are officers doing to catch those responsible?"
- **Answer: Power**

Explanation: Police hold legal power: they enforce the law. Asking what is being done about a problem is a question for a source of power.

### 1.2 A&E waiting times hit a record high this winter (Health, News)

- Source: Professor Grace Mensah, who studies NHS performance data
- Ask: "Why are waits getting longer, and how does this winter compare with previous years?"
- **Answer: Expertise**

Explanation: Academic expertise answers the ‘why’ and ‘how’ questions and puts the figures in context: is it getting better or worse?

### 1.3 What it’s like to start university as a care leaver (Education, Feature)

- Source: Kai, a second-year student who grew up in foster care
- Ask: "What was your first term like?"
- **Answer: Experience**

Explanation: Someone affected by the issue can put a human face to it and answer ‘What is it like?’. These sources are often the hardest to find.

### 1.4 Bus company cuts evening services across the region (Transport, News)

- Source: Gwen Pritchard, chair of the parish council in a village losing its last evening bus
- Ask: "How many residents have told you they can no longer get to work or appointments?"
- **Answer: Representative**

Explanation: A parish council is elected to speak for its village, so it can say how widespread the problem is. Bus cuts can hit rural areas hardest, where there may be no alternative, and those residents are easy to miss from a city newsroom.

### 1.5 River declared unsafe for swimming after sewage spill (Environment, News)

- Source: Tunde Afolabi, adaptive paddleboard instructor who was on the water when the discharge started
- Ask: "What did you see and smell that morning?"
- **Answer: Experience**

Explanation: An eyewitness describes what happened first hand. Witnesses are almost always quoted on details of description.

### 1.6 Abuse of referees in grassroots football reaches new high (Sport, News)

- Source: Priya Shah, head of discipline at the county football association
- Ask: "What sanctions will clubs face if their players or fans abuse officials?"
- **Answer: Power**

Explanation: Sports governing bodies have legal power: they make and enforce the rules. Power is not only political.

### 1.7 Tenants report black mould across council estate (Housing, News)

- Source: Winston Clarke, chair of the estate’s residents’ association
- Ask: "How many households have come to you with damp or mould problems?"
- **Answer: Representative**

Explanation: The residents’ association speaks for a wider group and can tell you how widespread the problem is, which one tenant cannot.

### 1.8 Patients in rural villages wait three weeks for a GP appointment (Health, News)

- Source: Dr Kevin Lam, GP and chair of the county’s local medical committee, which represents GPs
- Ask: "What are GPs across the county telling you about their workload?"
- **Answer: Representative**

Explanation: Local medical committees are elected to speak for GPs in their area. Dr Lam is a GP, but here he is asked about the experience of GPs as a group, not his own practice.

## Level 2: Same face, different role (2 pts each)

- Source: Lucy Kapoor, the local MP
- Ask: "How many constituents have contacted you about the closures?"
- **Answer: Representative**

Explanation: Here the MP is the voice of their constituents and has an ‘ear to the ground’. The council holds the power to close or save the libraries.

### 2.2 MPs speak out about the online abuse they receive (Politics, Feature)

- Source: Josh Bennett, an MP
- Ask: "What kind of messages have you received, and how has it affected you?"
- **Answer: Experience**

Explanation: In this story the MP is someone affected by the issue. They are asked what it is like, not what they will do about it.

### 2.3 Survival guide for the new intake of MPs (Politics, Feature)

- Source: Margaret Doyle, former chief whip who served 20 years in Parliament
- Ask: "What do new MPs most often get wrong in their first year?"
- **Answer: Expertise**

Explanation: Two decades in a senior role gives professional expertise in how Parliament works. They are offering insight, not taking action.

### 2.4 Town left without a single NHS dentist taking new patients (Health, News)

- Source: Imran Siddiqui, health minister
- Ask: "What is the government doing to bring dentists to the area?"
- **Answer: Power**

Explanation: The minister has responsibility for the sector and the power to spend money and change rules. They are asked about action.

### 2.5 Cup final preview: can the underdogs cause an upset? (Sport, Feature)

- Source: Mel Duggan, former footballer who played in two cup finals
- Ask: "How should the underdogs set up to stop their opponents’ midfield?"
- **Answer: Expertise**

Explanation: A former player can give expert insight into tactics. This is the expertise of achievement and long professional experience.

### 2.6 The day I walked out at a cup final (Sport, Feature)

- Source: Mel Duggan, former footballer who played in two cup finals
- Ask: "What does it feel like to walk out of the tunnel on final day?"
- **Answer: Experience**

Explanation: Same person, different question. Now she describes a first-hand experience rather than analysing tactics.

### 2.7 Campaign launched to save school libraries (Arts, News)

- Source: Femi Adeyemi, bestselling children’s author with two million followers
- Ask: "Will you use your platform to call on the government to protect library funding?"
- **Answer: Power**

Explanation: An author can be a voice of expertise, but here they are using cultural and mobilisation power to call for action.

### 2.8 Parents angry at parking fines outside primary school (Community, News)

- Source: Gareth Price, admin of a 12,000-member local parents’ Facebook group
- Ask: "What concerns are members raising most often?"
- **Answer: Representative**

Explanation: A group admin can summarise what members are saying, but does not formally speak for them the way a union does. Make that clear when you quote them.

### 2.9 Nurses at the city hospital vote on strike action (Health, News)

- Source: Marites Santos, nurse and union steward at the city hospital
- Ask: "How do your members feel about the latest pay offer?"
- **Answer: Representative**

Explanation: As an elected steward she speaks for union members on her site. She is a nurse, but the question is about members’ views, not her own.

### 2.10 Leaving home to nurse in the NHS: the international recruits keeping wards open (Health, Feature)

- Source: Marites Santos, nurse and union steward at the city hospital
- Ask: "What was your first winter on the ward like after arriving from the Philippines?"
- **Answer: Experience**

Explanation: Same person, different question. Now she is describing her own experience of moving to the UK to work, so she is an experience source.

## Level 3: Find the blind spot (3 pts each)

### 3.1 Pharmacies ration ADHD medication as shortage drags on (Health, News)

Already in draft:
- Power: Department of Health spokesperson says supply problems will be resolved by spring.
- Expertise: Pharmacology lecturer explains how manufacturing problems led to the shortage.

Prompt: The story explains what is being done and why. What is missing?

Options:
- Shoppers stopped outside a city-centre pharmacy. Ask: "What do you think about the medication shortage?" (Vox pop: Random passers-by have no particular power, expertise or experience of the issue.)
- **[CORRECT]** Ash Patel, who was diagnosed with ADHD at 34 and has gone three weeks without medication, found through an ADHD charity. Ask: "How has the shortage affected you?" (Best choice: Experience: Shows the human impact and answers ‘Why does this matter?’. Charities can often put you in touch with people willing to talk.)
- Your flatmate, who has an ADHD diagnosis. Ask: "Have you had trouble getting your prescription?" (Friend or family: A conflict of interest, and you can’t present them independently. Use your contacts to find someone else instead.)
- A second pharmacology academic at another university. Ask: "Why is there a shortage?" (Role already covered: The draft already has this expertise. A second expert adds little unless they disagree.)

Explanation: The draft has power and expertise but nobody affected. An experience source makes the story matter to readers, and a strong quote from them could become the headline.

Widen the net: Stories about ADHD often quote parents talking about their children. Adults with ADHD can speak for themselves, and many are diagnosed later in life. A charity or support group can help you reach them.

### 3.2 Shoplifting up 30% across the county, figures show (Crime, News)

Already in draft:
- Experience: Owner of a village shop and post office describes being threatened by a shoplifter last month.
- Expertise: Criminologist explains what is driving the rise in retail crime.

Prompt: The draft has context and colour. What question is left hanging?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Harry Singh Gill, police and crime commissioner. Ask: "What resources are being put into tackling retail crime across the county, including in rural areas?" (Best choice: Power: The commissioner sets police priorities and budgets. They can answer ‘What is being done?’ and give a right of reply.)
- Shoppers on the high street. Ask: "Do you feel safe shopping in town?" (Vox pop: Opinions from random passers-by don’t answer any of the story’s questions.)
- Linh Nguyen, who runs a nail salon in the same village. Ask: "Have you been targeted too?" (Role already covered: A second case study adds colour but doesn’t fill the gap: nobody is being held to account.)
- Your uncle, a retired police officer. Ask: "Why don’t police turn up to shoplifting any more?" (Friend or family: Family members are not independent sources, and a retired officer has no power over current policy.)

Explanation: Without a source of power the story never answers ‘What is being done?’. Where an authority is implicitly criticised, you also need to seek a right of reply.

Widen the net: Crime coverage tends to focus on cities. Ask the commissioner how the figures compare for rural and urban areas, and whether police response times differ.

### 3.3 Primary school to move to a four-day week amid staff shortages (Education, News)

Already in draft:
- Power: Headteacher says the change is the only way to keep classes covered.
- Power: Council confirms it has approved the timetable change.

Prompt: Two sources of power, both explaining the decision. Who is missing?

Options:
- Passers-by near the school gates. Ask: "Is a four-day week a good idea?" (Vox pop: People who happen to be walking past may not even have children at the school.)
- Education secretary. Ask: "What is the government doing about teacher recruitment nationally?" (Role already covered: Another source of power. The story already has two, and none of the affected families have a voice.)
- **[CORRECT]** Ryan Murphy, chair of the school’s parent council. Ask: "What have parents told you about the change?" (Best choice: Representative: Speaks for parents as a group and can say how widespread concern is. This is the reaction the story lacks.)
- Your cousin, who teaches at a different school. Ask: "Would you want a four-day week?" (Friend or family: Not independent, and not connected to this school.)

Explanation: The draft only has the people who made the decision. A representative provides the reaction that moves the story on, and could give it a new angle.

Widen the net: Parent councils don’t always reflect every family. Ask about parents who work shifts, who don’t speak English as a first language, or whose children have additional needs, and how the school is reaching them.

### 3.4 Why are so many girls quitting football at 14? (Sport, Feature)

Already in draft:
- Expertise: Researcher at a women’s sport charity explains the drop-off in the figures.
- Experience: A 15-year-old describes why she stopped playing for her club.

Prompt: The feature explains the problem and shows the impact. What is it missing?

Options:
- Comments under a club’s Instagram post about girls’ teams. Ask: "(Quoting comments directly)" (Unverified: Anonymous comments can’t be verified, and they don’t represent anyone. They might point you to sources, though.)
- Your younger sister, who gave up last year. Ask: "Why did you stop playing?" (Friend or family: Family members aren’t independent. Find case studies through clubs, charities or forums instead.)
- A second teenager who quit. Ask: "Why did you stop?" (Role already covered: Useful for colour, but the story already has experience. Nobody is asked what will change.)
- **[CORRECT]** Head of women’s and girls’ football at the national FA. Ask: "What is being done to keep teenage girls playing?" (Best choice: Power: The governing body has power to change rules, funding and facilities. It answers ‘What is being done?’.)

Explanation: Even a feature benefits from someone who can say what is being done. Here that is a sports governing body, which has legal and financial power over the game.

Widen the net: Drop-off may not be the same for every group of girls. Ask the researcher whether the figures can be broken down by ethnicity, disability, class or region, and whether that points to other voices the feature needs.

## Level 4: Widen the net (3 pts each)

### 4.1 Universities warn tuition fee freeze will force course closures (Education, News)

Situation: You need an independent expert on university finances. Your first search turns up three professors. All three are white men who are regularly quoted in the media.

Fact: In the UK, out of 164 university vice-chancellors, only two are Black. There are 165 Black professors in the UK, out of 23,515. Only 61 UK professors are Black women.

Prompt: What should you do next?

Options:
- Go with the first result. He’s the most experienced. (Default choice: He may be a good source, but defaulting to the most-quoted expert means the same voices are heard every time.)
- **[CORRECT]** Search diverse experts directories and the Society of Black Academics for researchers who work on university finance. (Best choice: Directories and representative networks exist to surface expertise that default searches miss.)
- Replace the expert with a Black student on your course, to make the story more diverse. (Tokenism: A student isn’t an expert on university finance. The aim is to find under-represented experts, not to swap expertise for identity.)
- Interview all three professors to be thorough. (Same voices: Three similar sources answer the same question from the same position.)

Explanation: Under-representation at the top of a field means the easiest experts to find are rarely the only ones. A wider search still finds expertise, and often a fresher angle.

### 4.2 A night on the beat: policing the city after dark (Crime, Feature)

Situation: The force press office offers to arrange interviews with officers. Your last three police stories all quoted non-disabled male officers.

Fact: Figures from 2014 show that just 1.9% of police officers had declared a disability. Across the UK, there are more than 6.9 million disabled people of working age, 19% of the working population (Disabled Living Foundation, cited in Everybody In). Many disabilities can’t be seen, and not everyone chooses to declare one.

Prompt: How can you hear from a wider range of officers?

Options:
- Accept whoever the press office offers. (Default choice: Press offices tend to put forward the same experienced spokespeople.)
- **[CORRECT]** Ask the press office, and also contact staff networks such as the Disabled Police Association for officers willing to talk about their shifts. (Best choice: Representative bodies inside a profession can connect you with people whose experience of the job is rarely heard.)
- Ask a disabled friend what they think of the police. (Friend, wrong role: Not independent, and they can’t describe a night on the beat.)
- Interview a disabled officer, but only ask about their disability. (Identity, not experience of the job: The feature is about the job. Ask them about their shift as you would any officer. Disability may come up, but it shouldn’t be the only thing you ask.)

Explanation: A small group within a profession is easy to miss through official channels alone. Representative bodies are a route to those voices, and the question still has to fit the story. As a disabled journalist puts it in Everybody In, disabled people are ‘spoken about, but not often listened to’. Most disabilities can’t be seen: an officer with Crohn’s disease, diabetes or hearing loss may work the same shifts as everyone else. A staff network can reach officers you could never pick out yourself.

### 4.3 Charities warn demand for food banks has doubled (Society, News)

Situation: You want a charity leader to speak for the sector. Your contacts book has three chief executives of large national charities.

Fact: According to the Association of Chief Executives of Voluntary Organisations (ACEVO), more than a sixth of British charity bosses have been privately educated, compared to around 7% of the wider UK population.

Prompt: How can you check whether your sector voices are representative, and find others?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Check LinkedIn for details of their education and memberships, and look through speakers at sector initiatives such as diversity and inclusion conferences for other leaders. (Best choice: Background details help you see patterns in your contacts, and sector events list leaders you might not otherwise find.)
- Assume charity leaders reflect the people their charities serve. (Assumption: The ACEVO figures suggest that isn’t safe, particularly on class.)
- Skip charity leaders and quote food bank users only. (Wrong role: Users bring experience, but the story still needs a representative who can speak to the scale of demand. Ideally have both.)
- Pick the biggest charity: it’s the most authoritative. (Default choice: Size isn’t the same as representativeness. Smaller, local or user-led organisations may be closer to the people affected.)

Explanation: Diversity isn’t only about what people look like. Class and education shape who leads organisations, and so who gets quoted as speaking for others.

### 4.4 Factory closure: what next for the town’s economy? (Business, News)

Situation: You ask a business school press office for an economist. They put forward the same male professor they always offer. Every expert you have quoted this week has been a man.

Fact: Monitoring by students at City University London found that male experts outnumbered women on flagship UK news and current affairs programmes by four to one. A campaign brought it nearer three to one. In law, expert witnesses, cabinet politics and academia, men outnumber women by about 2.5 to one (Everybody In).

Prompt: What should you do?

Options:
- Use him. There aren’t enough women economists to choose from. (Assumption: The figures suggest women are under-represented on air more than they are in the professions themselves.)
- **[CORRECT]** Ask the press office whether a woman economist could comment, and be ready to explain why the interview matters if she hasn’t done one before. (Best choice: Everybody In recommends asking for a woman if one isn’t put forward. Some women experts need persuading: over 10% in City research said they worried about seeming ‘pushy’.)
- Keep him as the expert, and balance the story with a vox pop of women shoppers. (Vox pops don’t count: Gender balance matters among experts and main protagonists, not just in vox pops.)
- Drop the expert, since you can’t get balance. (Losing the role: The story still needs expertise. The aim is to look more widely, not go without.)

Explanation: If the gap on air is wider than the gap in the professions, part of the cause is how journalists choose and book experts. Asking for a woman takes extra effort, and it is a habit you can build into every booking.

### 4.5 Meet the next generation of climate scientists (Science, Feature)

Situation: You want to interview early-career researchers. The university press office suggests two PhD students from the same lab.

Fact: In the three academic years from 2016-17 to 2018-19, UK research councils granted 19,868 funded PhD studentships, but only 245 were allocated to students of Black and mixed-Black heritage.

Prompt: Where else could you look?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Ask people who have written about the lack of diversity in science for recommendations, and look at researcher networks and events such as Queer in Academia. (Best choice: People who have written about the problem often know who to recommend, and events list speakers you can approach.)
- Stick with the press office: they know the university best. (Default choice: Press offices are useful, but they tend to suggest the same few people.)
- Post on social media asking for ‘diverse scientists’. (Too vague: A vague callout can feel tokenistic. Be specific about the expertise you need and use targeted networks.)
- Interview the lab’s professor instead. Students don’t count as experts. (Wrong source: Early-career researchers are the story here. Their research gives them expertise, and their experience is what the feature is about.)

Explanation: Under-representation in PhD funding feeds through to who becomes an expert later. Looking beyond the usual channels now helps you find the voices that will otherwise stay unheard.

### 4.6 Black Caribbean pupils excluded from school at higher rates, figures show (Education, News)

Situation: Your newsroom’s cuttings show the same community spokesman quoted on school exclusions, on policing, and on a housing row.

Fact: Everybody In cites a figure that 94% of UK journalists are white (Clive Lewis MP, 2016). It says Black contributors tend to fall into three groups: whoever happened to be available, the ‘Rent-A-Quote’, and the rare contributor who is spot on, who then gets asked about every issue.

Prompt: How should you source this story?

Options:
- Call him again. He’s reliable and always available. (Rent-a-quote: One person can’t speak for a community that is far from one homogenous group, and he may have no expertise in exclusions.)
- **[CORRECT]** Look for the people closest to the story: parents’ and community groups (via social media, community centres or the council’s directory), plus researchers who study exclusions. (Best choice: Community groups are run by people active in their communities, who can introduce you to others. Researchers add independent expertise.)
- Ask the newsroom’s only Black reporter to find someone. (Tokenism: Everybody In describes editors pulling non-white journalists onto these stories. Building contacts is everyone’s job.)
- Use the Department for Education statement and the figures. (Power only: That says what is being done, but nobody affected or representing families is heard.)

Explanation: Relying on one spokesman reduces a community to one voice. Building contacts with community groups takes time, but the book notes it builds trust and can lead to exclusive stories.

### 4.7 Hosepipe ban announced as reservoirs fall (Environment, News)

Situation: Every contact you have for the story lives within five miles of your city-centre newsroom.

Fact: About 17% of people in England live in rural areas (Defra). Most newsrooms, and so most reporters’ contacts, are in towns and cities.

Prompt: How do you reach people outside the city?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Contact the rural community council, the National Farmers’ Union and parish council clerks, and search village Facebook groups, for farmers, growers and residents affected. (Best choice: Representative bodies, parish councils and local online groups are the way in to rural communities.)
- Do a vox pop in a market town on Saturday. (Vox pop: Still random passers-by, just in a different place.)
- Stick with your city contacts. A ban is the same everywhere. (Assumption: The rules may be the same, but the impact isn’t: farms, growers and homes on private water supplies are affected differently.)
- Ask the water company press office to find you a rural customer. (Second-hand access: They may put forward a customer who suits their message. Find people independently.)

Explanation: Urban bias is often a matter of geography: reporters’ contacts sit near the newsroom. Rural sources take deliberate effort, and the story is often different there.

## Level 5: Check your habits (3 pts each)

### 5.1 Interest rates rise again: what it means for mortgage holders (Money, News)

Situation: You found Dr Funmi Adebayo, a housing economist, through a diverse experts directory. She has agreed to an interview.

Prompt: What should you ask her?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** “How much more will a typical mortgage cost, and who will be hit hardest?” (Best choice: Expertise: She’s in the story for her expertise, so ask about it.)
- “What’s it like to be a Black woman in economics?” (Identity, not expertise: That could be a good question for a different story. In this one she is your expert.)
- “Do you have a mortgage yourself, and how will you cope?” (Wrong role: This turns your expert into a case study. Find a mortgage holder for that.)
- “Can you suggest a more senior colleague for us to speak to?” (Second-guessing: You found the expertise you needed. There’s no reason to look past it.)

Explanation: Experts from under-represented groups are often asked only about being under-represented. Treat them as you would any other expert: the question decides the role they play.

### 5.2 Care homes say they can’t fill night shifts (Health, News)

Situation: You pre-interview Kerry Walsh, who has managed a care home for 15 years. She knows the sector inside out. A colleague says she has a strong regional accent and might not ‘sound authoritative’ on air.

Fact: In 2006 the Sutton Trust found that more than half of the UK’s top 100 journalists had been privately educated, up from 49% in 1986 to 54%. Private schools educate around 7% of pupils. Everybody In asks journalists: ‘Are you equating accent or manner with expertise?’

Prompt: What do you do?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Book her. Judge her on what she knows, not how she sounds. (Best choice: Expertise: Fifteen years running a care home is professional expertise. Her accent tells you nothing about it.)
- Find a professor of social care instead. (Accent bias: An academic could add context, but replacing a strong source because of how she speaks is the bias the book warns about.)
- Use her as a case study about her own working life, and find someone else to explain the sector. (Wrong role: This turns an expert into an experience source because of her accent.)
- Ask her to speak more ‘neutrally’ for the interview. (Accent bias: There is nothing wrong with how she speaks. The problem is the assumption.)

Explanation: When many journalists share similar backgrounds, it is easy to treat their own accent and manner as the sound of authority. Everybody In suggests counting how often you hear working-class people in the news apart from in vox pops.

### 5.3 Gene therapy trial offers hope for children with rare muscle disease (Science, News)

Situation: Your draft quotes four scientists, all men, on what the trial results mean. The only woman in the story is the mother of a child on the trial, talking about her hopes.

Fact: When science writer Ed Yong reviewed his 2016 stories, 24% of quoted sources were women and 35% of stories had no women at all. One gene-editing story quoted six men for their expertise and one woman, about her experience as a mother. Two of the technique’s pioneers are women (The Atlantic).

Prompt: What is the problem with this draft?

Options:
- There isn’t one. There’s a woman in the story, so it’s balanced. (Counting heads: Balance is about roles too. Here, men explain and the only woman feels.)
- **[CORRECT]** Women only appear as experience. Find women researchers in the field through databases such as Request a Woman Scientist or Diverse Sources, and keep the mother’s story. (Best choice: The parent is a strong experience source. The gap is in who gets to be the expert.)
- Cut the mother so the story stays focused on the science. (Losing a role: That removes the human impact and leaves the story all-male.)
- Lift a quote from a woman scientist’s interview with another outlet. (Second-hand: You can’t check the context or ask your own questions.)

Explanation: Diversity is about the roles people play as well as who appears. If women and minority sources are only heard as case studies while experts and officials are all men, the story still reinforces a stereotype.

### 5.4 Your editor asks: how diverse are the sources in your stories? (Newsroom, Practice)

Situation: You care about diversity and think your sources are fairly balanced. You have never counted them.

Fact: The Global Media Monitoring Project found in 2020 that women were just 24% of news subjects and sources. Ed Yong tracked his sources in a spreadsheet: within four months women were 50% of the voices in his stories, and it stayed between 42% and 61%. He estimates it adds about 15 minutes per piece.

Prompt: What should you do?

Options:
- Trust your instincts. You know you care about equality. (Passive concern: Yong assumed this too, until he counted and found 24%.)
- **[CORRECT]** Keep a simple record of who you contact and who you quote, and review it regularly. (Best choice: Yong calls tracking ‘a vaccine against self-delusion’. Reporters in a 2026 newsroom study said a review could ‘show me my patterns’.)
- Aim for 50/50 in your vox pops, where it’s easiest. (Vox pops don’t count: Balance matters most among experts and main voices, which is where it is usually missing.)
- Leave it to the newsroom’s diversity lead. (Passing it on: In the 2026 study, reporters said diverse sourcing often came down to individual choice because the newsroom ‘doesn’t always push for it’.)

Explanation: You can’t fix a pattern you can’t see. Counting is simplest for gender, but the same record can track ethnicity, disability, age, region and the type of source: official, expert or affected.

### 5.5 App company fined for leaking users’ health data (Technology, News)

Situation: You emailed four experts on data protection law: three men and one woman. All three men replied, the woman didn’t, and your story now has three male experts.

Fact: Early on, women were only 30% of the people Ed Yong contacted. He needed to contact about 1.3 men for each male quote, and about 1.6 women for each female quote. Washington Post reporter Anna Fifield found the women experts she contacted were ‘immediately available’ (CJR).

Prompt: What went wrong?

Options:
- Nothing. Women don’t reply as often, so it can’t be helped. (Excuse: The imbalance started before anyone replied.)
- **[CORRECT]** Too few women were contacted to begin with. Start from a balanced list, using databases such as SheSource or Informed Opinions, and contact a few more people than you need. (Best choice: If the list is unbalanced, the replies will be too.)
- Run the story and add a line saying no women were available. (Excuse: One unanswered email isn’t evidence that no women were available.)
- Quote the woman’s LinkedIn post about the case instead. (Unverified: A post isn’t an interview. You can’t ask your own questions or check what she meant.)

Explanation: Women in fields where they are a minority may get lots of requests, and Yong suspected some decline because they assume they aren’t the right fit. Contacting more women, and explaining why they are right for the story, closes the gap.

### 5.6 Man dies after police restraint in city flat (Crime, News)

Situation: It’s 4pm and the story is due at 6. You have a police statement and a quote from the police and crime commissioner. A colleague says calling official spokespeople is quicker than going out to the community.

Fact: In a 2026 study of a Canadian newsroom, reporters named time pressure, routine reliance on official sources, problems of access and a lack of newsroom emphasis as barriers to diverse sourcing. Police reports often start a story and set the terms in which it is understood (Adamson et al.).

Prompt: What is the best approach?

Options:
- Run with the official sources tonight and add others tomorrow. (Official framing: The first version sets the story’s framing, and here only the police are heard.)
- **[CORRECT]** Contact the family’s representative and local community groups now, and an independent expert on restraint. Keep building those contacts after the story. (Best choice: This adds experience, representatives and expertise. Contacts built in advance make the next deadline easier.)
- Ask people in the street near the flat what they think. (Vox pop: Random passers-by can’t tell you what happened or speak for the community.)
- Ask a colleague who shares the man’s ethnic background to make the calls. (Burden: Reporters in the study warned against relying on minority colleagues and sources to be ‘educators’. Building contacts is everyone’s job.)

Explanation: Trust with communities takes time, so it has to be built before a deadline, not during one. As one reporter in the study put it, the aim is to call ‘the people actually affected, not just the people in power’.

### 5.7 Government tightens visa rules for overseas care workers (Politics, News)

Situation: Your 400-word story quotes the immigration minister and an opposition MP. Your editor says care workers and employers can go in a separate piece next week.

Fact: A study of immigration coverage in Belgium, Germany, Italy and the UK (2013–14) found about half of articles gave voice to only one type of actor, and two-thirds presented only one viewpoint. Newspapers covered a wider range over time, and longer articles included more voices (Masini et al.).

Prompt: What should you do?

Options:
- Agree. Balance across the paper’s coverage is enough. (Spread too thin: Most readers see one article, not the whole run of coverage.)
- **[CORRECT]** Make room for a care worker on a visa, found through a union or migrant workers’ group, and a representative of care employers. (Best choice: Adds experience and representatives to a story that only has power.)
- Add a third politician from another party. (Same type of actor: Three politicians disagreeing is still one kind of voice.)
- Add a vox pop on immigration from the high street. (Vox pop: Opinions from passers-by don’t include the people affected by the policy.)

Explanation: Two politicians disagreeing can look balanced, but they are the same type of source. The people most affected by immigration policy are often the least heard in immigration news.

### 5.8 The women breaking into the building trades (Business, Feature)

Situation: A women in construction network puts you in touch with three tradeswomen. All three are white and in their thirties.

Fact: Kimberlé Crenshaw coined ‘intersectionality’ to describe how disadvantages overlap. She notes African American girls in the US are six times more likely to be suspended than white girls, a problem of both race and gender (IJNet).

Prompt: How can you make the feature more inclusive?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Look for tradeswomen of different ages, ethnicities and backgrounds, including through social media hashtags, and ask every interviewee how they want to be described, including pronouns. (Best choice: Searching more widely reaches women on the margins, and asking avoids misgendering or misdescribing anyone.)
- Use the word ‘womxn’ throughout to be inclusive. (Assumed language: Inclusive labels can still misrepresent people. Ask your sources how they identify.)
- Three is enough. The network chose them for you. (Default choice: Networks are a good start, but they may put forward their most visible members.)
- Ask each woman only about sexism on site. (Narrow framing: That’s one part of the story. Ask about their skills and work as well.)

Explanation: ‘More women’ can still mean the same kind of woman. Thinking intersectionally, across race, class, age, disability and sexuality, helps you find the voices that are missing even from a story about under-representation.

### 5.9 Thousands of pupils taught maths by teachers without a maths degree (Education, News)

Situation: Dr Tom Hargreaves, an education researcher, agrees to an interview. He tells you he is autistic and asks for your questions in advance by email, and to do the interview by video rather than by phone.

Prompt: What do you do?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Agree. Send the questions and set up the video call. (Best choice: Reasonable adjustment: Adjusting how you interview someone costs little, and questions in advance often get more accurate, considered answers.)
- Call him anyway. A live phone interview gets more natural quotes. (Ignoring the request: You risk losing the source and getting a worse interview.)
- Find a different expert, since this will take longer. (Exclusion: Dropping sources who ask for adjustments shuts neurodivergent and disabled experts out of the news.)
- Agree, and mention in the story that he is autistic. (Irrelevant detail: His autism isn’t relevant to a story about maths teaching. Only include it if it is relevant and he is happy for you to.)

Explanation: Reasonable adjustments are part of good interviewing. Some neurodivergent and disabled sources prefer written questions, a particular format or more time, and providing that widens the pool of experts you can use.

### 5.10 Your editor asks how many disabled people you quoted this year (Newsroom, Practice)

Situation: To answer, you go through photos of everyone you quoted and count anyone who uses a wheelchair, a cane or a hearing aid.

Fact: Across the UK, 19% of working-age people are disabled (Disabled Living Foundation, cited in Everybody In). Many disabilities, including chronic pain, mental health conditions, diabetes, hearing loss and long-term illness, can’t be seen.

Prompt: What is wrong with this method, and what should you do?

Options:
- Nothing. Counting photos is the only objective way. (Only what you can see: You will miss most disabled sources, and may wrongly count others.)
- **[CORRECT]** Record what sources tell you when it is relevant, and work with disabled people’s organisations so disabled people are heard in every PEER role. (Best choice: An audit can only count what people choose to share. Disabled people’s organisations can put you in touch with disabled experts, not just case studies.)
- Ask every source at the end of every interview whether they are disabled. (Intrusive: Asking without context can feel intrusive. Voluntary, anonymous source surveys are a better way to collect this.)
- Disability is too sensitive to track, so leave it out. (Not tracking: If you don’t track it, you can’t see the gap.)

Explanation: Most disabilities aren’t visible, so an audit based on appearance undercounts and reinforces a stereotype of what disability looks like. Track what people choose to tell you, and look for disabled sources in every role, not just in stories about disability.

### 5.11 State pension age to rise to 68 sooner than planned (Money, News)

Situation: You have booked Dr Rachel Moore, a pensions economist, through an experts directory. A colleague mentions that she is a trans woman and suggests asking her about it ‘for colour’.

Prompt: What should you ask her?

Options:
- **[CORRECT]** Ask about the pension age, and check how she would like to be described in the story, as you would with any source. (Best choice: Expertise: She is in the story for her expertise. Checking how every source wants to be described, including names and pronouns, is good practice.)
- Ask about her transition. Readers will find it interesting. (Identity, not expertise: It is irrelevant to the pension age, and it is her decision whether to talk about it.)
- Mention in her description that she is trans, to show the story is diverse. (Outing: Including someone’s trans status without relevance or consent can out them and expose them to abuse.)
- Swap her for another expert to avoid controversy. (Exclusion: There is nothing controversial about a pensions expert talking about pensions.)

Explanation: LGBTQ+ and trans experts are sometimes approached only to talk about being LGBTQ+ or trans. Where it isn’t relevant, treat them like any other expert.

## Level 6: Answer the story’s questions (4 pts each)

### 6.1 Knife crime arrests of under-18s double in two years (Crime, News)

Questions and correct source:
- What is it like for families affected? [Experience] → **Kevin Doyle, whose son was stabbed, and who now volunteers with a victims’ charity** (Ask: "How has your family’s life changed since the attack?")
- Why is this happening? [Expertise] → **Dr Anjali Rao, criminologist who studies youth violence** (Ask: "What explains the rise among under-18s?")
- What is being done about it? [Power] → **City mayor, who controls the violence reduction budget** (Ask: "What will you do to bring the numbers down?")
- How are young people in the city reacting? [Representative] → **Sam Okoro, elected chair of the city’s youth council** (Ask: "What are young people telling you about safety?")

Distractors:
- Teenagers approached outside a shopping centre. Ask: "Do you know anyone who carries a knife?" (Vox pop: Ethically risky with under-18s, and they represent nobody but themselves.)
- Crime novelist. Ask: "Why do you think young people carry knives?" (Wrong role for this story: Writing crime fiction isn’t expertise in youth violence.)
- Your friend’s younger brother, who is at a local school. Ask: "Is knife crime a problem at your school?" (Friend or family: Not independent, and a child you know personally.)

Explanation: A full news story needs all four roles here: a family for impact, an expert for context, the mayor for action, and an elected youth body for reaction.

Widen the net: Knife crime coverage has often focused on some communities more than others. Check that your choice of case studies and experts reflects what the figures show, rather than reinforcing a stereotype.

### 6.2 Living with long Covid, three years on (Health, Feature)

Questions and correct source:
- What does daily life look like with long Covid? [Experience] → **Marcus Bell, a nurse who has been unable to work since 2023, found through a long Covid support group** (Ask: "Talk me through a typical day.")
- What does the latest research say? [Expertise] → **Dr Leila Haddad, who leads a long Covid clinical trial** (Ask: "What have we learned about recovery?")
- What are patients telling support groups? [Representative] → **Founder of a national long Covid patient network** (Ask: "What are the most common problems members raise?")

Distractors:
- Wellness influencer who sells recovery supplements. Ask: "How can people recover faster?" (Wrong role for this story: A large following is cultural power, not expertise, and they have a financial interest.)
- People in a GP waiting room. Ask: "Do you know anyone with long Covid?" (Vox pop: Second-hand and random. Find people with direct experience.)
- Your aunt, who had Covid twice. Ask: "Do you still feel tired?" (Friend or family: Family, and having Covid is not the same as having long Covid.)

Explanation: A human-interest feature can leave out power and still work. It leads on experience, with research and a patient network to show this is not just one person’s story.

Widen the net: Ask the patient network who is coming to them and who isn’t. People in insecure or frontline jobs may be less able to join support groups, and less likely to be heard.

### 6.3 Premier League club announces 20% rise in ticket prices (Sport, News)

Questions and correct source:
- How have fans reacted? [Representative] → **Chair of the club’s independent supporters’ trust** (Ask: "What are your members saying about the rise?")
- Why has the club done this? (Right of reply) [Power] → **Club chief executive** (Ask: "Why are prices going up, and will you reconsider?")
- Can the club justify it financially? [Expertise] → **Football finance analyst who studies club accounts** (Ask: "Does the club need the extra revenue?")
- What does it mean for families who go every week? [Experience] → **Beverley Grant, season ticket holder of 30 years who takes her grandchildren, found via the supporters’ trust** (Ask: "Can you still afford to go?")
- Will the rise push out fans who already feel unwelcome? [Representative] → **Co-chair of the club’s LGBTQ+ supporters’ group** (Ask: "Are members worried the rise will make it harder to keep coming?")

Distractors:
- Replies under the club’s announcement on social media. Ask: "(Quoting the angriest replies)" (Unverified: Anonymous replies can’t be verified. Use them to find sources, not as quotes.)
- Your housemate, who supports a rival club. Ask: "What do you make of the price rise?" (Friend or family: Not affected, not independent.)
- Former player turned TV pundit. Ask: "Is this bad for the game?" (Wrong role for this story: Cultural power and opinion, but not the supporters’ voice and not financial expertise.)

Explanation: The supporters’ trust gives reaction, the chief executive gives a reply, the analyst tests the club’s claims, the season ticket holder shows the impact, and the LGBTQ+ supporters’ group speaks for fans who may already feel less welcome at matches.

Widen the net: A supporters’ trust isn’t the only fan group. Disabled supporters’ associations and women’s supporters’ groups may also have different concerns about price and access.

## Level 7: Build the interview list (5 pts each)

### 7.1 Make your money last until payday: the experts’ guide (Money, Feature)

Pick 3. Must cover: Expertise (only Expertise allowed). Any weak source fails the list.

- Debt adviser at a money advice charity. Ask: "What is the first thing people should cut back on?" → Expertise: Professional expertise from working with people in debt every day.
- Author of a bestselling budgeting book. Ask: "What is your simplest budgeting rule?" → Expertise: Non-academic expertise. Authors are often keen to talk.
- Economist who researches household spending. Ask: "Where does the average household’s money go?" → Expertise: Academic expertise adds evidence to the tips.
- Blogger who paid off £40,000 of debt and now writes about frugal living. Ask: "What habit made the biggest difference?" → Expertise: Experiential expertise: long, deep experience of the process.
- Chancellor of the Exchequer. Ask: "What are you doing about the cost of living?" → Power: Power, but an advice feature isn’t asking what is being done.
- Bank chief executive. Ask: "Will interest rates come down?" → Power: Financial power, but not the voice this guide needs.
- Shoppers in a supermarket. Ask: "How do you save money?" → WEAK (Vox pop): Readers want tips from people who know more than they do.
- Your parents, who are good with money. Ask: "Any tips?" → WEAK (Friend or family): Not independent, and no credentials to offer readers.

Explanation: Some stories only need one type of source. An experts’ guide is built from a range of expertise: professional, academic, authors and people with long experience.

Widen the net: Money advice often assumes readers have savings, a car or easy access to big supermarkets. Experts who work with people on low incomes, or in rural areas, can make the guide useful to more readers.

### 7.2 Rail operator cancels 400 trains in a week over driver shortages (Transport, News)

Pick 4. Must cover: Power + Expertise + Experience or Representative. Any weak source fails the list.

- Rail operator’s managing director. Ask: "Why were so many trains cancelled, and when will it improve?" → Power: The operator is responsible, so it must get a right of reply.
- Transport secretary. Ask: "Will you step in over the operator’s performance?" → Power: Political power to act on the operator’s contract.
- Commuter with ME who books passenger assistance, found via a passenger forum. Ask: "What happens when your train is cancelled at short notice?" → Experience: Shows the impact. For passengers who book assistance, a cancellation can mean missed help and long waits, not just lateness. Forums are a good place to find people affected.
- General secretary of the train drivers’ union. Ask: "Why are there not enough drivers?" → Representative: Speaks for drivers and can explain the shortage from their side.
- Head of the independent passenger watchdog. Ask: "How many complaints have you received this week?" → Representative: Represents passengers and can say how widespread the problem is.
- Transport academic who studies rail staffing. Ask: "Is this shortage a one-off or a long-term trend?" → Expertise: Independent context on whether things are getting better or worse.
- People waiting on the platform. Ask: "Are you annoyed?" → WEAK (Vox pop): Of course they are. That tells readers nothing new.
- Your dad, who commutes by train. Ask: "Has your train been cancelled?" → WEAK (Friend or family): Family members aren’t independent sources.

Explanation: A strong news story here needs someone held to account, independent context, and either a person affected or a group representing them.

Widen the net: Cancellations hit some passengers harder. Disabled passengers who book assistance, and rural commuters with one train an hour, may have a different story from city commuters.

### 7.3 Exam board error gave 2,000 students the wrong A-level grades (Education, News)

Pick 4. Must cover: Power + Experience + Representative or Expertise. Any weak source fails the list.

- The exam board. Ask: "How did the error happen and how will students be compensated?" → Power: Responsible for the error, so they need a right of reply.
- Exams regulator. Ask: "What action will you take against the exam board?" → Power: Legal power to enforce rules on exam boards.
- Student who lost her university place because of the error, found through her post about it on TikTok. Ask: "What happened when you got your results?" → Experience: Shows the impact. Verify her story with documents before you publish.
- Head teachers’ association. Ask: "How many schools have been affected?" → Representative: Speaks for schools and can say how widespread the problem is.
- Former chief examiner. Ask: "How do marking errors on this scale happen?" → Expertise: Professional expertise to explain the process.
- Celebrity who failed their A-levels and became a millionaire. Ask: "Do grades really matter?" → WEAK (Wrong role for this story): Cultural power, but irrelevant to whether the board got it wrong.
- Students on a university campus. Ask: "Do you trust exam results?" → WEAK (Vox pop): Random students who weren’t affected.
- Your younger brother, who is in year 12. Ask: "Are you worried about next year?" → WEAK (Friend or family): Family, and not affected.

Explanation: The board needs to reply, an affected student shows why it matters, and either a representative or an expert gives scale or explanation.

Widen the net: Errors like this can hit students differently. A lost university place may be harder to recover for students without family support or private tutors. Ask the head teachers’ association whether some schools were hit harder than others.

### 7.4 Council cuts funding for the city’s Pride event (Community, News)

Pick 4. Must cover: Power + Representative + Experience or Expertise. Any weak source fails the list.

- Council cabinet member for culture. Ask: "Why has the funding been cut, and will you reconsider?" → Power: The council made the decision, so it must get a right of reply.
- Chair of the Pride organising committee. Ask: "What will the cut mean for this year’s event?" → Representative: Speaks for the organisers and the people who take part.
- Volunteer who has run the Pride youth zone for five years. Ask: "What does the event mean to the young people who come?" → Experience: Shows what is at stake for the people involved.
- Academic who studies the economic impact of city events. Ask: "What does an event like Pride bring to the local economy?" → Expertise: Independent context to test the council’s reasoning.
- Chair of a local LGBTQ+ business network. Ask: "How much trade do your members do over Pride weekend?" → Representative: Another representative voice, speaking for businesses.
- Your friend, who goes every year. Ask: "Are you upset about the cut?" → WEAK (Friend or family): Not independent. Find attendees through the organisers or community groups instead.
- Replies under the council’s announcement. Ask: "(Quoting the angriest replies)" → WEAK (Unverified): Anonymous replies can’t be verified, and may include abuse.
- Shoppers in the city centre. Ask: "Should the council pay for Pride?" → WEAK (Vox pop): Random opinions don’t answer any of the story’s questions.

Explanation: The council needs a right of reply, the organisers speak for the event, and either someone involved or an independent expert shows what is at stake.

Widen the net: Pride organising committees don’t always reflect the whole community. Ask whether trans people, disabled LGBTQ+ people and LGBTQ+ people of colour are involved, and seek their views too.
